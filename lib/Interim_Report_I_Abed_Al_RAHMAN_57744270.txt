1. Introduction
Logistics is one of those complicated industrial fields out there with complex and tight-knit
working procedures. Due to its complex nature, most involved tasks can often be labelled as
“fragmented” (further elucidation follows in this paper). From a technological point of view, it
can be said that logistics coordinators from all levels of working hierarchy, rarely have access to
a single, unified platform where they could manage their daily task lists, monitor where a fleet
of vehicles are located at any given moment, or get route suggestions optimized by advanced
algorithms. This lack of a centralized, reliable digital system makes it challenging to run
logistical operations efficiently without hurdles related to time (and therefore impacting the
overall financial value for the involved businesses).
1.1 Problem Context and Industry Challenges
The repercussions of a non-coordinated, poorly optimized logistical operation is reverberated
across, at different levels of a typical supply chain, which includes loss of valuable time for a
given business, and customer dissatisfaction (two crucial factors). Such affects are, in fact, more
pronounced in the context of Hong Kong, which also happens to be one of the top logistical
hubs of the Asia-Pacific region (Hong Kong Productivity Council, 2022; Knight Frank, 2025;
Yuen et al., 2018), and one of the world’s most densely urbanized cities. Hong Kong’s
gridlocked streets, time-sensitive work culture, and the constant juggling of multiple vehicles all
culminate in severe operational headaches (Taniguchi et al., 2001). Besides just commercial
implications, the lack of a dependable logistics hub can even lead to real threats impacting
human safety. For example, employees working on-site may face physical dangers when
navigating unknown locations, encountering unexpected weather, or experiencing psychological
pressure because of poor planning and unrealistic deadlines (Apdullah et al., 2021). Moreover,
drivers tend to lose significant amounts of time waiting in traffic jams when there is a lack of a
proactive, dynamic digital tool that has the ability to react to changing road conditions and alert
the concerned person beforehand. Contemporary researchers have demonstrated that this

5

dynamic ability to adapt is paramount in achieving effective and efficient routing (Herrera et al.,
2010). All of these persistent concerns, point to a technical need for a centralized application
built specifically for Hong Kong's shipping industry (instead of a general purpose application
like Google Maps) that can handle both the efficiency problems, and the worker safety concerns
at a systematically deep level.
1.2 Proposed Solution: The OrderVista Application
OrderVista, the application platform described in this paper, is to be designed specifically to
tackle these problems as aforementioned. What is being proposed, is a system that combines
advanced web and mobile technologies into one package. It is meant to make work-life easier
for individual workers at smaller and mid-sized shipping companies in Hong Kong where access
to cutting edge technologies is often limited (Hong Kong Productivity Council, 2022), and who
are tasked to manage complicated routes with many stops every day. At the heart of OrderVista
sits a dual technical structure. On one side, managers get access to a web interface where they
assign tasks and monitor operations in real-time and also have the chance to track the workers,
and receive feedback from them as well. On the other side, workers would use a mobile app out
in the field that visually elucidates the optimal routes to be taken to reach each of their assigned
stops, and also get real-time updates from managers. This software design directly addresses
what operations researchers call the "Vehicle Routing Problem", or VRP. That is, drivers get
routes with multiple stops that have been carefully optimized to save time and cut fuel expenses
(Toth & Vigo, 2002).
1.3 Core Innovations and Technological Integration
One feature that distinguishes OrderVista is how it seeks to integrate a Large Language Model,
or LLM from the internet cloud. This would act as an intelligent assistant that non-technical or
less “tech-savvy” workers can talk to naturally. In fact, both workers and managers can pose
questions in everyday language and get answers right away using natural language processing.
Since LLMs, an advanced tech-innovation of the 21st century, have emerged only recently, its

6

usage in business-related environments seems to be on fairly unexplored grounds. Nevertheless,
some of the initial findings look very promising (Hostinger, 2025; Addepto, 2025; Wang et al.,
2025; Maiti, 2024). Reducing cognitive load is a central concern for any multimodal application
development, and as of present, it seems to be quite clear that AI-assistants (integrated LLMs)
are capable of simplifying workflows by allowing people access to complicated bits of
information through intuitive and conversational interfaces. This, inevitably reduces the overall
cognitive load on users (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023). Interestingly, developing and integrating
conversational AI assistants into industry logistics applications represents a departure from
traditional software practices (Clerk Chat, 2025; Gupshup, 2022; Aiola, 2025; Brown et al.,
2020; Krishnamoorthy et al., 2024).
1.4 Project Aims and Contribution
This paper essentially proposes a novel and technically innovative attempt in developing a

2.1 Path Optimization Algorithms
At the heart of logistical optimization tasks lies the vehicle routing problem (VRP), which is a
concept focused on the efficient allocation of delivery routes. This complex optimization puzzle
has challenged researchers for over 60 years (Dantzig & Ramser, 1959). Essentially, the VRP
attempts to technically compute the most optimized routes for operating multiple vehicles such
that they can serve customers living in different locations, with the sole target of minimizing
time as much as feasible. VRP is closely related to the famous MinMax algorithm, which in
logistics is often used to minimize the worst-case scenario, ensuring that no single vehicle
suffers an excessively high workload compared to others (Bertazzi et al., 2015; Carlsson et al.,
2006). When VRP is applied, what is ideally expected is that vehicles initiate their routes from a
central depot, attend to each assigned destination, and finally return. The goal is to do all of this
while traveling the shortest possible distance or spending the least amount of money (Toth &
Vigo, 2002). The Vehicle Routing Problem is also classified as an NP hard problem, meaning
that computational complexity increases exponentially with the number of customers. Owing to
these computing constraints, heuristic and metaheuristic approaches perform better than exact
algorithms in large scale real world applications (Laporte, 2009).
2.1.1 Foundational and Heuristic Approaches
Classic algorithms form the backbone of modern pathfinding. Dijkstra’s algorithm remains a
standard method for computing shortest paths in weighted graphs and is widely used in network

8

routing and logistics (Dijkstra, 1959). It grows the search outward from a source node and, when
all edge weights are nonnegative and represent quantities such as time or distance, it guarantees
an optimal route. That said, the multi-stop demands of a typical delivery schedule quickly
outgrow what Dijkstra’s algorithm can deliver on its own. Hence, the famous A* algorithm
addresses this gap by incorporating a heuristic estimate to indicate the remaining cost to a
desired destination location. This steers the search toward promising regions and trims wasted
exploration time (Hart et al., 1968). As a result, the A* algorithm performs especially well in
point-to-point navigation scenarios. It is a principle applicable from video games all the way up
to real-world road networks. However, in real logistical settings, rather than being definitive
solutions to path optimization problems, both Dijkstra's and A* algorithm typically serve only as
building blocks within broader vehicle routing problem frameworks (Irnich & Desaulniers,
2005). On the other hand, in column-generation approaches to VRP, the subproblem is typically
a shortest path problem with resource constraints (SPPRC), which also enforces time windows
and vehicle capacity during new route constructions, directly connecting to published SPPRC
literature (Irnich & Desaulniers, 2005).
2.1.2 Metaheuristic Approaches
To tackle the full scope of the VRP, researchers have increasingly turned to metaheuristic
approaches that can find near-optimal solutions in a reasonable amount of time. One prominent
example is Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), a probabilistic technique inspired by the foraging
behavior of ants. In ACO, artificial "ants" construct routes, depositing virtual pheromones that
influence subsequent ants, reinforcing shorter or more efficient paths over many iterations
(Dorigo & Gambardella, 1997). This method has been successfully applied to various VRPs,
including those with capacity constraints and time windows. Another powerful class of
algorithms is Genetic Algorithms (GAs), which simulate the process of natural selection. A GA
starts with a population of random solutions (routes) and evolves them over generations through
processes like selection, crossover, and mutation to produce progressively better solutions. GAs

9

have proven effective for a wide range of VRPs, including complex scenarios like the Green
VRP, which aims to minimize CO2 emissions instead of merely the distance (Erdoğan & MillerHooks, 2012). These advanced algorithms are essential for creating the sophisticated, multivehicle, multi-constraint route plans required by modern logistics operations.
2.2 Traffic Congestion Alert Systems
At this stage, traffic congestion alert systems need to come to the discussion as well because
effective route planning needs to stay flexible rather than locked into a predetermined path.
Since traffic networks don't behave predictably, patterns fluctuate constantly throughout each
day. In this context, congestion remains the most disruptive challenge in this area. What appears
to be an ideal route during planning can quickly deteriorate once vehicles begin accumulating
one after the other. So far, to address this concern, GPS and logistics systems have adapted by
incorporating live traffic feeds, allowing routes to change on the fly while keeping drivers
informed about developing delays (Jain et al., 2023).
2.2.1 Real-Time Data Collection with Floating Car Data (FCD)
A key technology enabling real-time traffic monitoring is Floating Car Data (FCD). FCD
systems collect anonymized, timestamped GPS location and speed data from a large network of
moving vehicles, such as those using navigation apps or cellular services. This data provides a
live, comprehensive view of traffic speeds and flow across the entire road network, unlike
stationary sensors which are limited to fixed locations (Herrera et al., 2010). By aggregating
FCD, systems can identify congestion as it occurs, calculate accurate travel times, and generate
traffic reports rapidly. This technology is the backbone of the live traffic features in major
navigation services and is critical for any system aiming to provide routes that are optimal in
current conditions.
2.2.2 Predictive Analytics and AI-Driven Traffic Management
Recent developments in traffic control go beyond responding to existing situations. In fact, they
work on anticipating what comes next. Hence, using AI alongside machine learning, advanced

10

platforms can now examine past records together with current activity to predict congestion
before it forms (Vlahogianni et al., 2014). Spotting patterns like predictable rush hour backups
or understanding how various weather situations influence flow allows these platforms to
redirect drivers ahead of trouble. Moreover, AI-based computer vision, frequently built using
architectures like YOLO (You Only Look Once), now identifies incidents including collisions
and disabled vehicles while they're happening (Redmon et al., 2016). After detection occurs,
warnings transmit automatically to drivers approaching the area and traffic management offices,
keeping movement smoother and reducing crash likelihood. This combination of prediction and
response defines what makes transportation infrastructure truly intelligent.

2.3 Offline Navigation Technologies
While real-time data is invaluable, a reliance on constant internet connectivity presents a
significant vulnerability. This is especially true in areas with poor or non-existent mobile
coverage. Hence, offline navigation technologies are designed to address this challenge ensuring
that routing remains functional even without a data connection (Harish & Meena, 2025). As a
result, this becomes one of the most critical features for logistical operations in diverse or
remote territories.
2.3.1 Map Data Storage and Management
The core of any offline navigation system is the pre-loading of map data onto the user's device.
Modern applications achieve this efficiently by dividing maps into small, manageable pieces
called “tiles”. Rather than storing bulky raster images, many systems use vector tiles, which
define geographic features as points, lines, and polygons. This approach significantly reduces

12

storage requirements and allows for smooth scaling, rotation, and dynamic styling on the client
device (OpenStreetMap Wiki, 2016). Advanced compression techniques and packaging formats
like MBTiles, which are essentially SQLite databases, further minimize the data footprint on the
device. By implementing intelligent cache management, such as a system that automatically
removes older or less-used map data, applications can provide a robust offline experience
without overwhelming the device's storage.
2.3.2 Positioning in GPS-Denied Environments
When offline, maintaining an accurate position is another challenge, particularly in "urban
canyons" or tunnels where GPS signals can be weak or lost entirely. To overcome this, advanced
offline systems employ sensor fusion and dead reckoning techniques (He & Chan, 2016). Dead
reckoning is a process of calculating one's current position by using a previously determined
position and advancing that position based on known or estimated speeds over elapsed time and
course. Smartphones are equipped with a variety of sensors, including accelerometers (to
measure movement) and gyroscopes (to measure orientation), which can be used to estimate the
device's trajectory in the absence of a GPS signal (He & Chan, 2016). While prone to drift over
time, it seems that this method can effectively bridge short gaps in GPS coverage, providing
continuous and reliable navigation guidance until a satellite signal is reacquired (Renaudin et al.,
2012).
2.4 AI Integrated Systems
Beyond optimizing physical routes and managing real-time data, the next frontier in logistics
technology lies in enhancing the cognitive interface between the human operator and the
complex digital ecosystem. While the systems discussed previously focus on calculating optimal
paths and navigating dynamic environments, a significant opportunity remains to reduce the
cognitive load on drivers and dispatchers. The integration of advanced Artificial Intelligence
(AI), particularly in the form of conversational agents (such as Large Language Models),
represents an important paradigm shift. This translates to possessing the ability from merely

13

providing data to delivering contextual intelligence in an intuitive format and digital
environment.
2.4.1 The Evolution of Conversational AI in Enterprise
The use of automated conversational agents, or chatbots, in commercial applications is not
entirely a new phenomenon. However, it can be observed that early iterations of the use of AI in
enterprise software were largely rule-based, limited to responding to a narrow set of preprogrammed commands and frequently failing when users deviated from the expected script
(Adamopoulou & Moussiades, 2020). However, the recent and rapid advancements in machine
learning and natural language processing (NLP) have given rise to a new generation of AIpowered conversational systems that are no longer constrained to the aforementioned problems.
In fact, these systems can comprehend intent, recall context from a conversation, and handle a
much wider array of complex, unstructured user queries, making them viable for dynamic, highstakes professional environments (Aman et al., 2025).
2.4.2 Large Language Models (LLMs) for Decision Support
At the forefront of this evolution are Large Language Models (LLMs), which are deep learning
models trained on vast datasets of text and code. Based on the highly effective Transformer
architecture, these LLMs possess an unprecedented ability to understand, summarize, generate,
and reason about human language (Vaswani et al., 2017). Their application in enterprise settings
is seen as a transformative step, capable of creating a new "economics of ideas" by dramatically
lowering the cost of accessing and formulating complex information (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023).
For a logistics professional, an integrated LLM can act as an intelligent co-pilot, translating
complex operational data such as traffic patterns, delivery schedules, and vehicle diagnostics
into simple and actionable advice delivered through human natural language.
2.4.3 Application in Logistics and Operational Management
It is important to note that the potential for AI to augment decision-making in supply chain
management and logistics is a well-documented field of research, with machine learning models

14

being used for everything from demand forecasting to warehouse optimization (Baryannis et al.,
2019). However, the specific application of a conversational LLM as a real-time support tool for
on-the-ground personnel is an emerging and highly innovative area. For a driver, this means
being able to ask questions such as, "What is the most fuel-efficient route to my next stop?" or
"Are there any reported delivery issues in the Tsim Sha Tsui area?" and then receive an instant,
context-aware answer from the involved AI assistant. For a dispatcher, it allows for high-level
queries like, "Which driver has the capacity to handle an urgent pickup near their current
location?". Hence, by integrating an LLM, a logistics platform evolves from a passive tool for
displaying information into an active partner in the decision-making process. This directly
addresses the need for a system that reduces stress and enhances the operational intelligence of
its users.
2.5 Summary and Research Gap Identification
The literature review presented in this paper confirms that the core components of the proposed
system are individually well-established fields of research. The Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP)
and its variants have been extensively studied, with a rich body of work on exact methods and,
more practically, heuristic algorithms like Dijkstra's and A* algorithm for finding optimal paths
(Dijkstra, 1959; Hart et al., 1968; Toth & Vigo, 2002). Modern AI-powered route optimization
further enhances these methods by incorporating real-time data such as live traffic and dynamic
delivery windows to improve efficiency (Taniguchi et al., 2001; Vlahogianni et al., 2014). The
use of mobile applications in logistics is also prevalent for functions like GPS tracking and fleet
management, although research suggests that Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) and user
experience are often secondary considerations in their design (Flachsbart et al., 2013).
Simultaneously, the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) in supply chain
management is an emerging and highly promising field (Maiti, 2024; Krishnamoorthy et al.,
2024). It is seen that the current research demonstrates the potential of LLMs to process
unstructured data, enhance decision-making, and provide intelligent support (Wang et al., 2025).

15

Microsoft, for example, has demonstrated using LLMs to explain the complex outputs of
optimization solvers to supply chain planners, bridging the gap between raw data and human
understanding (Li et al., 2024). This also highlights a trend toward using LLMs not just for
customer-facing support, but as an internal tool to augment the capabilities of operational staff.
However, the review reveals a distinct and critical research gap, which this project aims to
address directly. While existing literature extensively covers route optimization (Toth & Vigo,
2002; Laporte, 2009) and, separately, the rise of conversational AI (Adamopoulou &
Moussiades, 2020), there is a clear deficiency in research concerning the synergistic integration
of these technologies into a single, unified, and user-centric platform specifically for on-theground logistics personnel.
Therefore, the identified gaps can be summarized as follows:
1. Lack of Integrated Intelligence: Current logistics platforms are proficient at calculating
and displaying an optimal route. However, they lack an integrated intelligence layer that
allows a driver or manager to interact with, query, and understand the operational plan in
natural language.
2. Focus on High-Level Planning vs. On-the-Ground Support: Much of the research on
LLMs in logistics focuses on high-level strategic functions for planners at a desk (Li et
al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). There is a notable lack of focus on using LLMs as a realtime "co-pilot" for the mobile worker who is actively executing the logistics plan and
facing immediate operational uncertainties.
3. Fragmented User Experience: The current technological landscape often forces a
logistics operator to mentally bridge the gap between a map interface, a task list, and
communication channels. This fragmentation can increase cognitive load and work stress
(Apdullah et al., 2021), which are issues that are particularly acute in the high-pressure,
complex urban environment of Hong Kong, where last-mile delivery faces unique
challenges (Yuen et al., 2018).

16

Therefore, this project is positioned at the intersection of these domains. It moves beyond
treating routing algorithms and AI as isolated components and proposes a holistic system where
they are deeply integrated. The proposed application is designed to fill the discussed research
gap by creating a seamless platform that not only provides a mathematically optimized route but
also empowers the user with an intelligent, conversational interface to manage their tasks and
navigate the complexities of their environment with reduced stress and enhanced efficiency.
3. Methodology and Design Philosophy
This section outlines the methodological approach, design principles, scoped objectives,
research questions, and constraints that guide the development of the project described in this
paper, i.e. OrderVista. The overall emphasis is on pragmatic, iterative progress and defensible
design choices for the long run.
3.1 Development Methodology
The development of OrderVista will adhere to a lightweight, iterative methodology. This
approach structures progress into short, time-boxed cycles, each focused on delivering a vertical
and end-to-end slice of functionality (e.g., from order import to proof-of-delivery). This cyclical
process emphasizes incremental advancement and continuous refinement based on internal
review and testing. At the conclusion of each cycle, a self-review will inform the reprioritization
of the development backlog, explicitly targeting the mitigation of technical risks.
Features are packaged as vertical slices to maintain a small, demonstrable scope and to validate
integrated system behavior early. Technical debt and unresolved edge cases are systematically
logged for subsequent iteration. The sequencing of work is risk-focused, with initial cycles
prioritizing core and typically error-prone components of logistics systems, including data
modeling, access control, offline robustness, route sequencing orchestration, and media
handling. To ensure traceability and reproducibility, the project will employ version control via
GitHub.

17

3.2 System Design Goals
The architectural and design choices for OrderVista are guided by a set of non-functional
requirements that ensure the system is fit for its intended context. They include, but are not
limited to:
1. Usability: Interfaces are designed for clarity and efficiency under time pressure, featuring
glanceable screens, explicit next actions, minimal manual data entry, and predictable
workflow patterns.
2. Pragmatic Scalability: The initial target supports the operational scale typical of small
and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Architectural choices, such as stateless
components and efficient geospatial queries, are preferred to facilitate future growth
without requiring fundamental redesign.
3. Reliability: Core application flows are engineered to tolerate intermittent connectivity.
This is achieved through mechanisms like local queuing of actions on the mobile client,
idempotent API operations, and deterministic rules for conflict resolution upon resynchronization.
4. Security and Privacy: The system enforces role-based access control and data
partitioning to ensure tenant isolation. Media access is managed via pre-signed URLs
with short validity periods. While transport-layer encryption is a baseline assumption, the
design also considers simple data retention and optional redaction policies to minimize
privacy exposure.
5. Maintainability: A modular architecture delineates clear boundaries between the mobile
client, backend API, routing orchestration logic, and media services. To aid
troubleshooting, the system incorporates basic observability through structured logging
and the propagation of correlation identifiers across services.

3.3 Scoped Objectives for IR1
The objectives for the first implementation review (IR1) are framed as pragmatic and achievable
outcomes, expressed as implemented approaches rather than performance or final-product
guarantees. These include:
1. O1: Driver Mobile MVP: To provide drivers with a minimal viable product (MVP)
encompassing authenticated access, a detailed task list, a basic map view, local queuing
for offline operations, and barcode/QR scanning integrated with photo capture for proofof-delivery.
2. O2: Backend and Data Model: To define and implement a multi-tenant data schema with
integrated role-based access controls, event auditing, presigned media upload
capabilities, and a simple mechanism for real-time status propagation.
3. O3: Route Sequencing and Persistence: To implement a baseline route sequencing
method, such as a nearest-next algorithm with optional refinement, or integration with an
accessible optimization API. The resulting routes, stop sequences, and estimated timings
will be persisted for execution and review.
4. O4: Telemetry and Geofencing: To establish a pipeline for ingesting device location
updates and applying proximity-based geofencing rules to suggest or automatically
advance task statuses (e.g., 'arrived' or 'completed'), with provisions for manual
dispatcher override.
5. O5: Dispatcher Portal MVP: To deliver a web-based dispatcher portal supporting CSV
order import (with integrated geolocation lookup), task management, tabular route, stop
visualization, live status filtering, and an interface for reviewing proof-of-delivery
evidence.
6. O6: Observability and Demonstration: To instrument basic logging and metrics for
critical system paths and to produce a coherent, end-to-end demonstration flow that
validates the IR1 scope.

3.4 Research Questions (RQs)
The following research questions (RQs) are formulated to guide the subsequent evaluation of
OrderVista. The IR1 implementation will establish the baselines and instrumentation necessary
for their comprehensive measurement in later stages.
1.

RQ1 (Routing Efficiency): To what extent does the implemented sequencing approach
reduce total route duration compared to a simple nearest-next baseline for urban delivery
routes comprising 10 to 20 stops?

2.

RQ2 (System Responsiveness): What are the observed 95th percentile (p95) latencies for
route optimization requests (≤20 stops) and for typical CRUD/API operations within the
system?

3.

RQ3 (Offline Robustness): Under planned disconnection intervals (e.g., approximately
10 minutes), are task states and user interactions reliably preserved and synchronized to a
consistent state within a reasonable timeframe after abrupt network reconnections?

4.

RQ4 (Proof-of-Delivery Integrity): Under typical lighting conditions, what is the success
rate of the barcode/QR scanning function? If OCR is attempted, what is its indicative
extraction accuracy on a small and defined set of package labels?

So far, the scope for IR1 has been deliberately bounded to ensure a tractable and high-quality
deliverable.


In-Scope: The system will handle single-depot, single-shift routing scenarios,

The deliverable includes an Android/iOS driver client, a dispatcher web interface,
offline-first workflows, and barcode/QR-based proof-of-delivery with photographic
evidence.



Assumptions: The project assumes reliable access to geocoding services and road
network data for Hong Kong, that mobile devices will permit background location

4. System Architecture

OrderVista integrates mobile, web, and cloud to synchronize drivers, dispatchers, and
management. The core architecture consists of a cross-platform mobile app (Flutter), real-time
web dashboard (React.js), and a Supabase cloud backend, with Edge Functions connecting to
both mapping/optimization (Mapbox) and conversational AI (OpenAI GPT-4o).
This section further delineates the comprehensive system architecture and technical design for
OrderVista. The design adopts a modern, decoupled paradigm centered on a Backend-as-aService (BaaS) core to accelerate development while ensuring robustness, scalability, and
security
4.1 High-Level System Architecture
At its core, OrderVista is structured into three primary layers:
1. Client Layer: Comprises a Flutter mobile application for drivers and a React.js web
dashboard for dispatchers and managers.
2. Backend Layer: Built on Supabase, providing a suite of integrated services: JWT-based
authentication, a PostgreSQL database with PostGIS extension for geospatial operations, file
storage, and real-time channels.
3. External Services Layer: Integrates specialized third-party APIs, including Mapbox for
mapping, directions, and route optimization, and OpenAI for the LLM-based intelligent co-pilot.
Clients communicate directly with Supabase for standard CRUD operations and authentication.

21

For specialized tasks, such as traffic-aware multi-stop routing or LLM interactions, clients
invoke Supabase Edge Functions. These functions securely call external APIs and persist results
back to the database. This architecture provides a clear separation of concerns, enhances security
by keeping secrets server-side, and accelerates development for a solo developer.
The high-level system architecture is depicted in Figure 1.

Figure 1: OrderVista High-Level System Architecture
4.2 Technology Stack Justification
The selection of technologies is primarily driven by the requirements for performance, developer
efficiency, and ecosystem support. The “tech-stack” chosen is further elaborated as follows:
1.

Frontend - Flutter & React.js: Flutter was chosen for the driver mobile app due to its
high-performance native compilation, expressive widget framework, and robust
plugin ecosystem for critical features like maps and geolocation. React.js was
selected for the dispatcher web dashboard due to its dominant web ecosystem and
component-driven architecture, which enables the rapid assembly of complex, datarich interfaces.

2.

Backend - Supabase: Supabase provides an industrial-strength foundation, offering
PostgreSQL with first-class geospatial capabilities via PostGIS, secure built-in

22

authentication, Row-Level Security (RLS) for multi-tenancy, and real-time
subscriptions. This significantly reduces the overhead of developing and maintaining
custom backend infrastructures.
3.

Serverless Logic - Supabase Edge Functions: These Deno-based functions are ideal
for encapsulating sensitive operations (e.g., API key usage), compute-heavy tasks,
and multi-step workflows. They provide a secure environment for server-side
validation and interaction with external services.

4.

AI & Mapping - OpenAI & Mapbox: The OpenAI GPT-4o API provides state-ofthe-art conversational reasoning for the intelligent co-pilot. Mapbox also offers
customizable vector maps, traffic-aware routing, and a dedicated Optimization API
suitable for solving Vehicle Routing Problems (VRP), with strong SDK support for
both Flutter and web.

4.3 Data Model
The data schema, implemented in PostgreSQL with PostGIS, models the core entities of a
logistics operation. It is designed for multi-tenancy, with a “company_id” on all operational
tables and Row-Level Security (RLS) policies enforcing data isolation. Key entities include:
1.

Companies & Users: Extends Supabase's auth.users to manage organizations and their
members with specific roles (e.g., driver, dispatcher, admin).

2.

Vehicles & Tasks: Represent the assets to be managed and the jobs to be completed.
The tasks.location field is a PostGIS geography type for spatial queries.

3.

Routes & Route_Tasks: The routes table defines a journey for a vehicle and driver,
while route_tasks acts as a join table specifying the optimized sequence of tasks,
including calculated ETAs and travel distances.

4.

Telemetry: Continuously records vehicle and driver location data for live tracking and
geofencing, with a spatial index on the location field for high-performance proximity
searches.

4.4 API and Security Design
The API strategy is two-pronged, mainly leveraging the strengths of the Supabase platform.
1.

Auto-Generated CRUD API: The Supabase client libraries (supabase-flutter,
supabase-js) provide a type-safe, auto-generated API for all database operations.
Security is enforced at the data layer via PostgreSQL RLS policies. For example, a

24

policy may ensure a driver can only read tasks and update the status of tasks assigned
to their active route.
2.

Custom Business Logic via Edge Functions: For operations involving external APIs
or complex orchestration, custom Edge Functions are used. These functions validate
the incoming JWT to ensure the user is authenticated and authorized, execute the
business logic, and interact with the database.

Optimization API, and persists the resulting route.
2. POST /chat-interaction: Fetches relevant user context (e.g., active route, tasks)
and queries the OpenAI API to generate a grounded, contextual response for the
co-pilot.

prevent role escalation or data leakage.

offline resilience, and an intelligent LLM co-pilot. The design provides a clear and maintainable
blueprint for development, from the MVP to a production-ready system.

5. Implementation Plan and Milestones
This section delineates the structured approach for implementing the core capabilities of
OrderVista within the scope of Interim Report 1 (IR1). The plan adopts a pragmatic, iterative
methodology that emphasizes incremental development of vertical, end-to-end feature slices.
The primary focus is on establishing foundational robustness in critical areas. This includes data
security, offline functionality, and route sequencing, while ensuring the delivery of a
demonstrable and coherent prototype. Milestones are framed as achievable targets that reflect
the project's intent and methodological approach.
5.1 Work Breakdown and Timeline
The following implementation trajectory is organized into seven sequential phases, each
designed to build upon the last and reduce the overall technical risk early in the development
cycle.
1. Phase A: Foundations and Access Control will establish the core system infrastructure.
This involves initializing the code repositories, setting up continuous integration checks
(i.e. via GitHub), and defining the essential data entities (tenants, users, vehicles, tasks,
routes). A central outcome of this phase is the implementation of role-based access
control and data partitioning to ensure multi-tenant security from the outset. The result
will be a secure and authenticated system with seeded development data and a minimal
dispatcher interface for entity management.
2. Phase B: Driver App MVP focuses on developing the core of the mobile application.
This phase will implement an offline-first architecture, featuring a local task cache, an
outbox for queuing actions during disconnection, and the integration of barcode/QR
scanning with local photo capture. The outcome will be a functional driver application
that can view and update tasks reliably, even with intermittent connectivity.
3. Phase C: Eventually, dispatcher Ingest and Management shifts focus to the web
dashboard. This phase will enable dispatchers to import orders via CSV with integrated

29

geocoding, provide full task CRUD (Create, Read, Update, Delete) capabilities, and
establish real-time status propagation. The result is an operational dispatcher portal
capable of managing the order-to-task lifecycle.
4. Phase D: Route Sequencing and Persistence introduces the first layer of optimization.
This phase will implement a baseline sequencing algorithm, such as a nearest-next
heuristic, and persist the resulting routes, stop orders, and estimated arrival times. The
outcome is a complete round-trip flow: from order import to sequenced route
assignment, which is visible to both the dispatchers and the drivers.
5. Phase E: Telemetry and Geofence Automation enhances the system with situational
awareness. By ingesting device location updates and computing proximity to task
locations, the system will suggest or automatically advance task statuses based on
conservative rules, with provisions for dispatcher override. This phase delivers
automated status transitions backed by traceable audit logs.
6. Phase F: Proof-of-Delivery and Media Handling formalizes the completion of tasks. It
will implement a secure, presigned upload flow for images, attach proof-of-delivery
(PoD) artifacts to tasks, and store barcode scan data. The outcome would be a secure,
end-to-end PoD process that is reviewable within the dispatcher portal.
7. Phase G: Stabilization and Demonstration constitutes the final preparation for IR1. This
phase involves adding structured logging and metrics for critical paths, exercising a
complete end-to-end smoke test, and preparing all necessary demonstration artifacts,
including a script and supporting documentation.
5.2 Verification and Acceptance Criteria
Each phase concludes with verifiable checks that serve as pragmatic acceptance indicators,
ensuring the project remains on track and meets its technical objectives. The idea is further
elaborated as follows:

Foundations: The data model must enforce tenant partitioning; seed scripts must
successfully create example data; and basic audit trails must be functional.

2.

Driver App MVP: The application must display tasks from cache, successfully
synchronize queued updates after a disconnection, and locally capture barcode and photo
data.

3.

link expiration.

7.

Stabilization: A final, full “smoke-test” must validate the integrated workflow from
import to PoD, with operational logging and a prepared demonstration script.

5.3 Testing Strategy
The testing strategy is designed to provide confidence in the system's reliability while respecting
the constraints of a single-developer project. It focuses on critical paths and reproducibility.
Unit-level testing will validate core data transformations, such as geocoding parsing and
distance calculations, and verify the correctness of authorization rules. Integration testing will
exercise key cross-component flows, including media uploads, the telemetry-to-geofence event
chain, and the CSV import process. Finally, a scripted end-to-end smoke test will validate the
complete user journey, from dispatcher import and assignment to driver execution and proof-of-

31

delivery, including a scenario with simulated network disruption. This will be supported by
tooling including version control, basic CI pipelines, seeded datasets, and a telemetry simulator.
5.4 Risk Mitigation
The plan presented in this paper proactively addresses common risks in logistics application
development with pragmatic mitigation strategies. Here are some of those main risk mitigation
strategies:
1.

External Service Dependency: Variability in geocoding, mapping, and optimization APIs
will be mitigated by caching results, maintaining a simple local sequencing fallback, and
implementing rate limiting.

2.

Offline Conflict Resolution: Potential data conflicts during re-synchronization will be
managed through deterministic merge rules, clear conflict flags, and simple correction
workflows within the dispatcher UI.

3.

Data Privacy: Security for handled media will be enforced via presigned URLs with
short time-to-live (TTL), tenant-scoped storage paths, and configurable data retention
policies.

4.

Scope Management: To guard against schedule pressure, development will prioritize
end-to-end functionality over non-essential features, deferring advanced UI polish and
treating Optical Character Recognition (OCR) as an exploratory, non-blocking stretch
goal.

5.

Device Limitations: Challenges with location accuracy and background operation will be
addressed by using conservative geofencing thresholds, providing manual status
overrides, and maintaining clear documentation for required device settings.
6. Preliminary Evaluation Plan

This section outlines the methodology for the preliminary evaluation of OrderVista within the
scope of Interim Report 1 (IR1). The plan is designed to be pragmatic and repeatable, focusing
on establishing measurable baselines and instrumentation for the system's core capabilities. The

32

evaluation will assess performance against key criteria which includes routing efficiency, system
responsiveness, offline robustness, and proof-of-delivery integrity. All of this has to be done
using synthetic datasets and scripted procedures suitable for a single-developer project. The
foundational work presented in this paper will enable more comprehensive measurement and
field testing in subsequent project phases.
6.1 Evaluation Objectives and Metrics
The evaluation is structured around four primary objectives, each with defined metrics that align
directly with the research questions posed in the methodology. They are as follows:
1. Routing Efficiency: The primary objective is to quantitatively assess the performance of
the implemented route sequencing algorithm against a established baseline. The key
metrics are the relative reduction in total route duration (percentage) compared to a
simple nearest-next baseline for test sets of 10-20 stops, and the average deviation in
minutes from estimated arrival times. Evidence will be generated through route
summaries, comparative tables, and visualizations of the planned paths.
2. System Responsiveness: This objective seeks to characterize the latency of the system
under light load conditions. Metrics include the 95th percentile (p95) latency for
common CRUD API endpoints (e.g., task creation, status updates), the p95 latency for
optimization requests handling up to 20 stops, and the completion time for small image
uploads via the presigned URL flow. Data will be gathered from structured server and
client logs with high-resolution timestamps.
3. Offline Robustness: The goal is to empirically observe the system's behavior during
planned network disconnections and its subsequent recovery. Success will be measured
by the task update preservation rate (the percentage of user actions successfully
synchronized after a ~10-minute disconnection), the time to consistency postreconnection, and the count and resolution outcome of any data conflicts. Evidence will
be collected from logs of scripted disconnection scenarios.

33

4. Proof-of-Delivery Integrity: This objective is to validate the reliability of the PoD
process under typical operational conditions. Metrics include the success rate of
barcode/QR scans under standard indoor lighting, the success rate and timing of image
uploads, and the availability of PoD records for dispatcher review within a short
timeframe. If Optical Character Recognition (OCR) is prototyped, its precision, recall,
and F1 score on a small sample set will be calculated indicatively.
Additionally, lightweight Reliability Indicators will be monitored throughout all tests, including
crash-free driver sessions and API error rates, to provide a baseline assessment of system
stability.
6.2 Datasets, Baselines, and Test Scenarios
To ensure repeatability and control, the evaluation will utilize synthesized artifacts and
established baselines.
1. Synthetic Order Sets: Two to three synthetic datasets representing urban delivery scenarios in
Hong Kong will be generated. These sets, comprising 10, 20, and 50 stops with plausible
coordinates, time windows, and service durations, provide a controllable and privacy-preserving
method to exercise the full system workflow.
2. Baseline Methods: The primary baseline for routing efficiency will be a nearest-next (greedy)
sequencing algorithm using Haversine distance. This establishes a clear, minimal benchmark
against which the performance of more advanced sequencing (e.g., a local search heuristic or
external API) can be compared.
3. Telemetry Simulation: A basic replay tool will be developed to simulate device location
updates along planned routes. This allows for the reproducible testing of geofencing and status
automation without the logistical overhead of initial field trials.
4. Barcode and PoD Samples: A small corpus of generated barcode and QR labels, along with
package photos taken under typical lighting conditions, will be used for PoD integrity tests.

34

6.3 Evaluation Procedures
Moreover, structured, scripted procedures will be employed to ensure the consistency and
repeatability of all tests.


Routing Efficiency: A script will load a synthetic order set, generate a route using the
baseline method, then generate a route using the OrderVista system. The total duration,
distance, and schedule adherence of both routes will be recorded and compared.



System Responsiveness: A short load-simulation script will perform bursts of CRUD
operations, invoke the optimization endpoint, and upload small images. Client and server
timestamps will be used to compute percentile latencies.



Offline Robustness: A controlled test will involve starting a route on the mobile app,
disabling network connectivity for approximately ten minutes while updates are made,
and then reconnecting to observe the synchronization process, measuring its duration and
identifying any conflicts.



Proof-of-Delivery Integrity: This procedure will involve repeated scanning of sample
barcodes, followed by the capture and upload of PoD images. If OCR is implemented, its
output on a labeled image set will be compared to ground-truth data.

6.4 Instrumentation, Analysis, and Reporting
Instrumentation will be implemented through structured logging with correlation IDs across
client and server components, alongside minimal metric counters for key system endpoints. Data
handling will adhere to privacy conscious principles, using tenant-scoped test data, short-lived
access links for media, and the anonymization of any visual evidence used in reporting.
Analysis and reporting will emphasize clarity and traceability. For each objective, a compact
summary of metrics will be presented alongside a qualitative interpretation of the results, noting
observed bottlenecks or stability concerns. Supporting visuals will include route plots from the
dispatcher dashboard and latency distribution charts. The report will explicitly acknowledge
the limitations of this preliminary evaluation, including the use of synthetic data, simplified

35

traffic assumptions, and controlled load conditions, while highlighting how the established
instrumentation paves the way for more rigorous evaluation in future work.
6.5 Risk Mitigation
The proposed evaluation plan incorporates mitigations for several identified risks as follows:
1. Synthetic-to-Real Gap: The use of realistic urban coordinates and time windows, coupled
with clear documentation of assumptions, will help bridge the gap between simulation
and real-world operation.
2. Device Variability: Tests will specify device settings and be repeated under consistent
conditions, with device models and OS versions logged to contextualize results.
3. External Service Dependence: Caching of geocoding results and the retention of a local
sequencing baseline will ensure the evaluation can proceed reliably.
4. Limited Sample Sizes: The focus will be on demonstrating repeatable procedures and
observable trends rather than producing statistically significant point estimates, with a
plan to expand sample sizes in subsequent project phases.
6.6 Conclusion
This interim report establishes the practical development path toward OrderVista, a secure,
offline-capable, AI-assisted logistics platform specifically designed for Hong Kong. A
significant gap in the current landscape is identified: the absence of a unified, user-focused
solution that integrates VRP-based routing, real-time situational awareness, and conversational
guidance. The proposed architecture, comprising Supabase/PostGIS, Edge Functions, Mapbox,
and an LLM-powered copilot, is constructed to address these needs. Interim Report I emphasizes
demonstrable vertical integration, with evaluation targeting routing efficiency, responsiveness,
offline reliability, and proof-of-delivery data integrity. With risk controls implemented, the
immediate priorities are to complete task sequencing, strengthen security monitoring, and
systematically enhance both the copilot and dynamic re-optimization functions, moving the
platform from its minimum viable product stage toward production readiness.
